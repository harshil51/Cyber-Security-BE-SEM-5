1. CYBERCRIME AND SOCIAL MEDIA — STRUCTURE AND OBJECTIVES 
1.1 What is Cybercrime? 
Cybercrime refers to illegal activities in which computers, networks, digital devices, or the 
Internet are used as a tool, target, or environment for committing a crime. 
Examples 
• Hacking  
• Identity theft  
• Online fraud  
• Phishing  
• Cyberstalking  
• Malware attacks  
• Ransomware  
• Data theft  
• Account takeover  
• Online harassment  
Simple definition for exam 
Cybercrime is any unlawful activity carried out using computers, digital devices, communication 
networks, or the Internet. 
 
1.2 What is Social Media? 

=== PAGE 3 ===
Social media refers to online platforms that allow users to create, share, communicate and 
interact with digital content. 
Examples: 
• Facebook  
• Instagram  
• X  
• LinkedIn  
• YouTube  
• WhatsApp  
• Telegram  
• Snapchat  
 
1.3 Relationship Between Cybercrime and Social Media 
Social media provides criminals with a huge environment containing: 
• Personal information  
• Photos  
• Contact information  
• Location information  
• Social connections  
• Professional information  
• User interests  
• Public conversations  
This information can be misused for cybercrime.

1.4 Structure of Cybercrime on Social Media 
The social-media cybercrime ecosystem generally contains: 
1. Victim 
The person or organization targeted by the criminal. 
2. Attacker 
The individual or group attempting to commit the crime. 
3. Platform 
The social-media service through which communication or activity occurs. 
4. Information 
Information collected from profiles, posts, messages, photos, etc. 
5. Attack mechanism 

=== PAGE 5 ===
The technique used by the attacker. 
Examples: 
• Phishing  
• Fake profiles  
• Malicious links  
• Social engineering  
• Account takeover  
6. Criminal objective 
The final goal of the attacker. 
Examples: 
• Money  
• Personal information  
• Account access  
• Revenge  
• Espionage  
• Fraud  
 
1.5 Objectives of Cybercrime on Social Media 
1. Financial gain 
Attackers may attempt to steal money through: 
• Fake investment schemes  
• Shopping fraud  
• Payment scams  
• Account takeover  
2. Identity theft 
Personal information can be used to impersonate a victim. 

=== PAGE 6 ===
3. Credential theft 
Attackers may attempt to obtain: 
• Usernames  
• Passwords  
• Authentication information  
4. Privacy violation 
Private photographs, conversations or personal information may be obtained or exposed. 
5. Cyberstalking 
Repeated unwanted monitoring or contact can be performed through social platforms. 
6. Reputation damage 
Fake posts or accounts may be used to damage someone's reputation. 
7. Malware distribution 
Malicious links or files can be distributed through social platforms. 
8. Misinformation and manipulation 
Social media can be used to distribute false or misleading information. 
 
1.6 Why Social Media is Attractive to Cybercriminals 
• Large number of users  
• Huge amount of personal information  
• Easy communication  
• Fake accounts can be created  
• Users often trust familiar-looking profiles  
• Information can be collected through public posts  
• Rapid information sharing  
• Global reach  
Conclusion 

=== PAGE 7 ===
Social media provides significant benefits for communication and information sharing, but its 
large user base and availability of personal information also create opportunities for 
cybercriminal activities. 
 
2. SOCIAL ENGINEERING 
2.1 Definition 
Social engineering is the manipulation of people into performing an action or revealing 
information that compromises security. 
The attacker generally exploits human psychology rather than only technical vulnerabilities. 
Important line for exam 
Social engineering attacks target the human element of security. 
 
2.2 Basic Social Engineering Process 
Information Gathering 
        ↓ 
Target Selection 
        ↓ 
Building Trust 
        ↓ 
Manipulation 
        ↓ 
Victim performs action 
        ↓ 
Information / Access obtained 
        ↓ 
Criminal objective 
 

=== PAGE 8 ===
2.3 Why Social Engineering Works 
Attackers commonly exploit psychological factors such as: 
• Trust  
• Fear  
• Curiosity  
• Urgency  
• Authority  
• Greed  
• Helpfulness  
• Social pressure  
For example: 
"Your account will be blocked unless you verify it immediately." 
The attacker creates fear + urgency, encouraging the victim to act without checking the 
message. 
 
2.4 Evolution of Social Engineering 
Social engineering has evolved alongside communication technology. 
Stage 1 — Traditional social engineering 
Attackers manipulated people through: 
• Telephone calls  
• Face-to-face communication  
• Letters  
Stage 2 — Email-based attacks 
With the growth of email, criminals began using deceptive messages to obtain information. 
Stage 3 — Web-based attacks 
Fake websites and online services became common attack mechanisms. 

=== PAGE 9 ===
Stage 4 — Social-media attacks 
Attackers started using: 
• Fake profiles  
• Direct messages  
• Fake advertisements  
• Social-media impersonation  
Stage 5 — Mobile-based attacks 
Smartphones introduced: 
• SMS scams  
• Fake mobile applications  
• Messaging-platform scams  
Stage 6 — AI-assisted social engineering 
Modern attacks can use automation and AI to create highly convincing: 
• Messages  
• Fake profiles  
• Voice impersonation  
• Images  
• Emails

3. TYPES OF SOCIAL ENGINEERING ATTACKS 
 
3.1 PHISHING 
Definition 
Phishing is a social-engineering attack in which an attacker pretends to be a legitimate person 
or organization to trick a victim into revealing information or performing an unsafe action. 
Common targets 
• Passwords  
• Banking information  
• Account credentials  
• Personal information  
Common characteristics 
• Fake identity  
• Urgent message  
• Suspicious link  
• Fake login page  
• Request for sensitive information  
Example 
A victim receives: 

=== PAGE 11 ===
"Your account requires immediate verification." 
The message contains a link to a fraudulent website designed to imitate a legitimate service. 
Types of phishing 
Email phishing 
Fraudulent emails are sent to victims. 
Spear phishing 
A phishing attack specifically targeted at a particular person or organization. 
Whaling 
A targeted attack against high-level individuals such as executives. 
Smishing 
Phishing through SMS/text messages. 
Vishing 
Phishing through voice calls. 
Social-media phishing 
Phishing through social-media messages or fake profiles. 
Prevention 
• Verify the sender  
• Don't blindly click links  
• Check website addresses  
• Use MFA  
• Never share passwords  
• Use password managers  
• Report suspicious messages  
 
3.2 PRETEXTING 
Definition 

=== PAGE 12 ===
Pretexting is an attack in which the attacker creates a false story or situation, called a pretext, to 
convince the victim to provide information or perform an action. 
Example 
An attacker pretends to be: 
"IT support" 
and claims that the employee's account has a technical problem. 
The attacker attempts to convince the employee to reveal sensitive information. 
Key idea 
Pretext = Fake story 
Characteristics 
• Fake identity  
• Fake scenario  
• Trust building  
• Information request  
Prevention 
• Verify identity independently  
• Follow organizational procedures  
• Never disclose confidential information based only on a phone/email request  
 
3.3 BAITING 
Definition 
Baiting uses something attractive or interesting to tempt a victim into performing an unsafe 
action. 
The "bait" may involve: 
• Free software  
• Free rewards  
• Interesting files  

=== PAGE 13 ===
• Fake offers  
• Suspicious physical devices  
Example 
A person receives a message: 
"Congratulations! You have won a reward. Click here." 
Curiosity causes the person to interact with the malicious content. 
Key word 
Baiting = Temptation 
Prevention 
• Avoid unknown downloads  
• Don't accept unknown devices  
• Verify offers  
• Use security software  
 
3.4 QUID PRO QUO 
Definition 
Quid pro quo means "something for something." 
The attacker offers a supposed benefit or service in exchange for information or access. 
Example 
An attacker pretends to be technical support and offers to "fix" a computer problem in exchange 
for account information. 
Key concept 
Attacker offers benefit 
          ↓ 
Victim accepts 
          ↓ 

=== PAGE 14 ===
Victim provides information/access 
Difference from baiting 
Baiting Quid Pro Quo 
Uses temptation Uses exchange 
"Take this reward" "Give me this and I will give you something" 
Victim is attracted Victim expects a benefit 
 
3.5 TAILGATING / PIGGYBACKING 
Definition 
Tailgating is an unauthorized physical entry into a restricted area by following an authorized 
person. 
Example 
An unauthorized person follows an employee through a secure door. 
Authorized employee 
        ↓ 
Secure door 
        ↓ 
Unauthorized person follows 
        ↓ 
Restricted area 
Tailgating vs Piggybacking 
Tailgating: Unauthorized person enters without explicit permission. 
Piggybacking: The authorized person may knowingly or unknowingly allow the other person to 
enter. 
Prevention 
• Access cards  

=== PAGE 15 ===
• Security guards  
• Visitor verification  
• Turnstiles  
• Security awareness  
• Don't hold secure doors open for unknown people  
 
3.6 IMPERSONATION 
Definition 
Impersonation occurs when an attacker pretends to be another person or trusted entity. 
Examples: 
• Fake employee  
• Fake customer-support agent  
• Fake friend  
• Fake company representative  
• Fake social-media account  
Objective 
To gain: 
• Trust  
• Information  
• Money  
• Access  
Prevention 
• Verify identity  
• Use official communication channels  
• Don't trust profile pictures alone  
• Use MFA  

=== PAGE 16 ===
 
3.7 SCAREWARE 
Definition 
Scareware is a technique that uses fear or alarming warnings to convince victims to perform an 
action. 
Example 
A fake popup says: 
"Your computer is infected! Click immediately to fix it." 
The user may be encouraged to download malicious software or provide information. 
Psychological principle 
Fear 
 ↓ 
Urgency 
 ↓ 
Reduced thinking 
 ↓ 
Unsafe action 
Prevention 
• Don't trust unexpected security warnings  
• Use legitimate security software  
• Close suspicious popups  
• Keep software updated

5. CYBERSTALKING 
5.1 Definition 
Cyberstalking is the repeated use of digital communication technologies to monitor, harass, 
threaten, intimidate, or otherwise unwantedly target an individual. 
It may occur through: 
• Social media  
• Email  

=== PAGE 19 ===
• Messaging platforms  
• Forums  
• Online communities  
 
5.2 Characteristics 
• Repeated unwanted contact  
• Monitoring online activities  
• Harassing messages  
• Threatening communication  
• Fake profiles  
• Spreading personal information  
• Tracking online activity  
 
5.3 Types of Cyberstalking Behavior 
1. Harassment 
Repeated unwanted messages or communication. 
2. Monitoring 
Repeatedly observing someone's online activities. 
3. Impersonation 
Creating fake accounts pretending to be the victim. 
4. Threatening behavior 
Sending threatening or intimidating messages. 
5. Information gathering 
Collecting personal information from public sources. 
6. Defamation 
Posting false or damaging information. 

=== PAGE 20 ===
7. Identity misuse 
Using another person's identity or information online. 
 
6. DIRECT CYBERSTALKING 
Definition 
Direct cyberstalking involves direct communication or interaction with the victim. 
Examples: 
• Repeated unwanted messages  
• Threatening emails  
• Repeated calls  
• Direct messages  
• Harassing comments

7. INDIRECT CYBERSTALKING 
Definition 
Indirect cyberstalking involves monitoring, influencing, or targeting the victim without 
necessarily communicating directly with them. 
Examples: 
• Monitoring public activities  
• Creating fake accounts  

=== PAGE 21 ===
• Collecting personal information  
• Contacting people connected to the victim  
• Spreading information about the victim

   ↓

Direct Indirect 
Direct contact with victim May occur without direct contact

9. PREVENTION AND PROTECTION AGAINST CYBERSTALKING 
1. Privacy settings 
Restrict who can see personal information. 
2. Block the stalker 
Use platform blocking features. 
3. Report the account 
Report abusive or threatening behavior. 
4. Preserve evidence 

=== PAGE 22 ===
Keep: 
• Screenshots  
• URLs  
• Messages  
• Dates and times  
• Usernames  
5. Strengthen accounts 
Use: 
• Strong passwords  
• Unique passwords  
• MFA  
6. Reduce personal information 
Avoid publicly sharing: 
• Phone numbers  
• Home addresses  
• Real-time locations  
• Sensitive personal details  
7. Inform trusted people 
Tell trusted family members, friends, educational institutions, or employers where appropriate. 
8. Contact authorities when necessary 
Threatening or serious cases should be reported to appropriate law-enforcement/cybercrime 
authorities.

14. CHALLENGES IN CYBERCRIME INVESTIGATION 
This is a very important 10–15 mark question. 
1. Anonymity 
Attackers may hide their identity using different technical and online mechanisms. 
2. Jurisdiction 
The attacker, victim, server and evidence may be located in different countries. 
Attacker → Country A 
Server   → Country B 
Victim   → Country C 

=== PAGE 29 ===
This creates legal and investigative challenges. 
3. Encryption 
Encrypted communication can make evidence difficult to obtain or interpret. 
4. Large volume of data 
Investigators may need to examine: 
• Emails  
• Logs  
• Messages  
• Files  
• Devices  
• Network traffic  
5. Volatile evidence 
Some digital evidence can disappear or change quickly. 
6. Attribution 
Finding out who actually performed the attack can be difficult. 
7. Multiple identities 
Criminals may use: 
• Fake accounts  
• Multiple usernames  
• Compromised accounts  
8. Anti-forensic techniques 
Attackers may attempt to hide or destroy evidence. 
9. Rapidly changing technology 
Cybercriminal methods and technologies continuously evolve. 
10. International cooperation 
Investigations may require cooperation between: 

=== PAGE 30 ===
• Law-enforcement agencies  
• Service providers  
• Organizations  
• Different countries  
Conclusion 
Cybercrime investigation requires technical expertise, proper evidence preservation, legal 
authority, digital-forensics capabilities and cooperation between multiple organizations and 
jurisdictions. 
 
15. BOTNETS 
15.1 Definition 
A botnet is a network of compromised Internet-connected devices that can be controlled by an 
attacker. 
The infected devices are commonly called bots or zombies. 
Basic structure 
             Botnet Controller 
                    | 
          +---------+---------+ 
          |         |         | 
         Bot       Bot       Bot 
          |         |         | 
       Device    Device    Device 
The devices can potentially be used together for malicious activities. 
 
15.2 Botnet Architecture 
Two common architectural concepts are: 
1. Centralized architecture 

=== PAGE 31 ===
Bots communicate with a centralized command-and-control system. 
       C2 Server 
       /  |  \ 
      /   |   \ 
   Bot   Bot   Bot 
Advantage for attacker 
Centralized management can make coordination straightforward. 
Weakness 
If the central infrastructure is disrupted, many bots may lose communication. 
 
15.3 Decentralized / Peer-to-Peer Architecture 
Bots communicate through multiple nodes instead of depending on one central server. 
Bot ↔ Bot ↔ Bot 
 ↕         ↕ 
Bot ↔ Bot ↔ Bot 
Advantage 
There is no single central point that necessarily controls all communication. 
Challenge 
Disrupting such a network may be more difficult. 
 
16. EVOLUTION OF BOTNETS 
Early botnets 
Initially, botnets were relatively simple networks of compromised computers. 
Development 
They evolved to include: 
• Automated control  

=== PAGE 32 ===
• Larger numbers of devices  
• More resilient communication  
• Multiple types of Internet-connected devices  
IoT era 
Botnets increasingly targeted: 
• Routers  
• Cameras  
• IoT devices  
• Other Internet-connected devices  
Modern botnets 
Modern botnets can potentially contain large numbers of compromised devices and may be 
used for multiple criminal purposes. 
 
17. BOTNET CAPABILITIES AND CRIMINAL APPLICATIONS 
At a high level, botnets can be used for: 
1. Distributed denial-of-service attacks

Bot 4 ──┤

2. Spam distribution

3. Malware distribution

Compromised systems may be used to steal information. 
5. Cryptocurrency-related abuse 
Compromised computing resources may be misused for unauthorized computation. 
6. Proxying/traffic routing 
Compromised devices may be used to route malicious traffic.

How to write a 10–15 marks answer 
For almost every topic in this unit, follow this structure in the exam: 
1. Definition — 1–2 marks 
Give a precise definition. 
2. Introduction — 1 mark 
Explain the topic in 2–3 lines. 
3. Types/Classification — 3–4 marks 
Explain each type with a small example. 
4. Working/Structure — 2–3 marks 
Draw a flow diagram. 
5. Example/Case Study — 2 marks 
Give one realistic example. 
6. Prevention/Impact — 2–3 marks 
Write 5–7 points. 
7. Conclusion — 1 mark 
End with 2–3 lines. 
This structure can comfortably turn a short topic into a complete 10–15 mark answer. 
If your exam is tomorrow, the highest-priority topics to prepare in full-answer format are: 
Social Engineering & its types, Cyberstalking, Cybercrime Taxonomy, Social Media as a 
Cybercrime Ecosystem, Cybercrime-as-a-Service, Challenges in Cybercrime Investigation, 
Botnet Architecture & Applications, and Attack Vector Classification.
